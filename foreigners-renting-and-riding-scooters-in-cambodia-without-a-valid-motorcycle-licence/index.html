Foreigners Renting and Riding Scooters in Cambodia Without a Valid Motorcycle Licence

For foreigners in Cambodia, the legal picture is narrower than the rental market makes it look — and the biggest risk isn't just a police fine. It's the gap between what rental shops hand over and what insurers or courts recognize after a crash.

By AG Cambodia May 20, 2026

Executive Summary

For foreigners in Cambodia, the legal picture is narrower than the rental market makes it look. Under Cambodia's Road Traffic Law, riders of motorcycles under 125cc do not need a Cambodian driving licence, but riders of 125cc and above do. Foreigners who lawfully live in Cambodia and wish to drive are directed by Article 45 to obtain a Cambodian licence unless a bilateral or multilateral recognition arrangement applies. In practice, this means the strongest legal position for a foreigner is either riding a sub-125cc scooter or converting a home licence into a Cambodian one through the Ministry of Public Works and Transport (MPWT).

The biggest risk is not just a police fine. It is the gap between what rental shops will hand over and what insurers or courts will recognize after a crash. Recent travel-advisory and insurer material shows that many hire companies have no insurance, travel insurers often restrict motorcycle claims unless the rider holds the correct motorcycle entitlement, and local motor policies commonly exclude driving "without a valid license" or, in one Cambodian insurer's wording, without a valid Cambodian licence.

That means a foreigner can easily be rented a scooter that is easy to ride, easy to pay for, and hard to defend legally or financially after an accident.

Recent road-safety data underscore why this matters. UNICEF Cambodia reports that road crashes in 2024 still caused 2,844 crashes, 1,509 deaths, and 3,720 injuries. In 2025 reporting, motorcyclists remained the overwhelming share of road deaths, and speed, right-of-way violations, wrong-side driving, and overtaking were leading causes. For a foreign renter, the safest recommendation is simple: if you are not clearly licensed and insured for the bike you intend to ride, do not rely on the fact that a rental shop is willing to rent it to you.

Legal Baseline in Cambodia

Cambodia's Road Traffic Law draws the central legal line at 125cc. Article 8 says drivers of vehicles with engine capacity from 125cc upward must hold a driving licence; for vehicles below 125cc, drivers are not required to hold a licence, though they must be at least 15 and carry an identity document. Article 40 places motorcycles from 125cc upward in Category A. MPWT's public-service page also states broadly that every driver in Cambodia needs a valid licence, while its FAQ confirms that a car licence does not authorize a motorcycle above 125cc.

For foreigners who are resident

For foreigners who are resident rather than merely passing through, Article 45 is the decisive provision: unless Cambodia recognizes their licence through an agreement or international convention, foreigners lawfully living in Cambodia and wishing to drive must either take the Cambodian test or exchange their national licence through MPWT. The exchange requires a passport, valid visa, proof of address, medical certificate, and an embassy-certified translation if the licence is not in English or French.

MPWT's public-service centres include Aeon Mall Phnom Penh, Aeon Mall Sen Sok, provincial offices, and online intake through the driver-licence portal. You can email them at info@mpwt.gov.kh.

Documents needed to exchange your licence

  • Passport & Visa — a valid "Ordinary" or Business Visa
  • Proof of Address — a residence certificate or letter from your local Sangkat (commune) office
  • Medical Certificate — a standard health check verifying fitness to drive
  • Translated Licence — embassy-certified translation if not in English or French
  • Passport Photos — typically 3 white-background 4×6 cm photos for in-person applications

Penalties are less transparent in English than the underlying legal structure. Article 75 says driving without a licence triggers a provisional penalty under sub-decree, and repeated no-licence driving can escalate to criminal penalties for some drivers, but the law expressly says that the repeat-offender imprisonment clause does not apply to motorcycles, motorcycles with trailers, or tricycles.

Separately, Cambodia's demerit system deducts points for "failure to meet driving licence requirements," and repeated use of an invalid or inappropriate licence can lead to larger deductions and eventual invalidity of the licence. The exact current roadside fine amount for motorcycle no-licence violations was not clearly available in the official English sources reviewed.

As an insurance broker, we need to remind everyone: even if your vehicle (motorbike or car) is insured, should you have an accident with an invalid driver's licence, the insurance will not cover you.

Police powers are also narrower than many travelers assume. The Road Traffic Law allows police to suspend licences in specified cases, keep vehicles after accidents, and temporarily keep certain vehicles for listed violations, but it also says police may not confiscate licences or vehicle papers outside the cases defined by law, and illegal confiscation by police is itself punishable. That matters because anecdotal reports of "bike confiscation" should not be treated as automatic or universally lawful for ordinary licence issues.

Recognition of Foreign Licences

The law itself does not publish a simple country-by-country list in English. Instead, it says recognition depends on bilateral or multinational agreements or international conventions. One treaty-level clue is the ASEAN agreement on recognition of domestic driving licences, which states that ASEAN states recognize domestic licences other than temporary, provisional, or learner permits. But public-facing guidance for foreigners remains inconsistent.

That inconsistency is important. The UK's FCDO says that to hire a motorcycle over 125cc, travelers may sometimes need the 1949 International Driving Permit plus their home licence, and that long-term rental or private driving requires a Cambodian licence. By contrast, the Australian Embassy in Phnom Penh states that foreigners wishing to drive in Cambodia must hold a Cambodian driver licence and that international licences are not accepted, while also noting that no licence is required for motorcycles up to 125cc. A commercial self-drive operator claims Cambodia accepts the 1949 IDP, but that is not the same as clear, official Cambodian foreigner guidance.

The safest analytical conclusion is this: for sub-125cc scooters, Cambodian road law itself is the strongest basis for legality; for 125cc and above, the strongest short-term documentary package reflected in current English-language guidance is a home motorcycle licence plus a 1949 IDP, but enforcement and roadside understanding may still be inconsistent; for residents, a Cambodian licence is the least contestable option.

Quick decision guide

  • Short-term visitor, bike under 125cc — lower legal risk under road law; still confirm your travel insurer covers motorcycle riding
  • Short-term visitor, 125cc or above — carry a home motorcycle licence plus a 1949 IDP; expect inconsistent enforcement and recognition
  • Resident, any engine size — convert to a Cambodian licence or take the Cambodian test through MPWT
  • Always — carry a helmet, your ID, the vehicle registration card, and the rental contract

Rental Market Practice in Phnom Penh and Tourist Areas

The rental market clearly clusters around the legal threshold. Siem Reap rental sites prominently advertise 110cc and 125cc scooters, while also listing some 150cc models such as the Honda PCX 150. Another Cambodia-based operator explicitly markets that no driver's licence is needed for motorbikes under 125cc and quotes deposits ranging from $300 to $900 depending on model. This pattern strongly suggests that operators know the 125cc threshold shapes customer demand.

What shops actually check is often looser than the law. A recent Cambodia hostel guide wrote that small-scooter rental is usually "pay, leave a passport copy or deposit, grab the key," with no one asking about a licence. Recent Reddit and Cambodia Expats Online discussions tell a similar story: tourists and expats report that for small scooters, roadside attention is often more focused on helmets, mirrors, lights, and obvious violations than on licence verification, especially in tourist-heavy areas such as Siem Reap. These are anecdotal reports, not legal authority, but they align with the commercial rental behavior.

Insurance and Liability Exposure

Insurance is where informal practice turns into real financial exposure. Cambodia's insurance framework does provide compulsory motor third-party liability insurance, but the official insurance texts reviewed apply that requirement primarily to business-use vehicles, commercial transport, company vehicles, NGO/international-organization vehicles, and similar categories — not to every private scooter on the street. Travel advisories therefore caution that many motorcycle hire companies in Cambodia do not have insurance, and that riders may personally bear damage, loss, or injury-to-others costs after an accident.

Even when insurance exists, licence status matters. Etiqa Cambodia's private vehicle policy excludes driving without a valid licence, while Dara Insurance's product summary excludes a driver who has no valid Cambodian licence. At the same time, Cambodian road law exempts sub-125cc riders from needing a licence. That creates a real ambiguity: a rider may be lawful under traffic law but still face an insurer argument that coverage fails because the rider lacked the insurer's required licence status. For foreigners, that is a material risk, especially if their home licence does not include motorcycle privileges.

Liability to third parties is also not something to assume away. Cambodian insurance law requires insured commercial vehicles to compensate third parties for bodily injury or death, but if the rental bike is uninsured or the policy is voided or excluded, the rider can find the claim landing directly on them. The Road Traffic Law also places drivers under criminal law for driving-related offenses, and vehicle owners under civil liability when their vehicles cause accidents.

Enforcement and Safety Risk

On enforcement, the most defensible conclusion is that Cambodia operates on a mix of formal law and uneven roadside practice. The FCDO notes on-the-spot helmet fines; Smartraveller says speeding tickets should be paid at payment centres and emphasizes that drivers should have a valid licence and confirmed insurance. Recent forum reports suggest that in tourist zones, checks often target visible infractions first. That does not make no-licence riding safe; it means the legal and financial consequences often arrive only when a crash occurs.

The safety backdrop is severe. UNICEF Cambodia reports that 2024 still saw 1,509 deaths and 3,720 injuries from road crashes. A 2025 road-safety profile put Cambodia's road-fatality rate at 18.8 per 100,000. In 2025 reporting, roughly three-quarters to four-fifths of road deaths were motorcyclists, and among killed motorcyclists a large share were not wearing helmets. Recent local reporting attributes about half of crashes to speed, with right-of-way violations, wrong-side driving, and overtaking next. Night travel is also repeatedly flagged as higher risk.

Practical Guidance for Foreigners

If your nationality is unspecified, the most prudent advice is nationality-neutral. If you will ride only a sub-125cc scooter for a short stay, you are on the strongest footing under Cambodian road law, but you should still verify whether your travel insurer covers you for motorcycle riding and whether your home licence gives you motorcycle entitlement. If you want to ride 125cc or above, do not assume a rental counter's willingness proves legality; carry a valid home motorcycle licence and, at minimum, the 1949 IDP reflected in UK guidance, understanding that Australian guidance conflicts and roadside recognition may vary. If you will live in Cambodia and drive regularly, exchange your licence or sit the Cambodian test through MPWT.

Do not leave your passport as a deposit unless you have no viable alternative and fully trust the operator; both UK and Australian travel advisories caution against it. Carry a helmet, your identity document, the vehicle registration card, the rental contract, and whichever driving document is strongest for your case. If your need is transport rather than riding itself, ride-hailing services such as Grab and PassApp are available in Phnom Penh, Siem Reap, and Sihanoukville and usually present a lower-risk option than self-riding.

The table below summarizes how legal requirements, licence recognition, rental reality, and insurance outlook line up across the most common situations foreigners face.

Tourist with home car licence only, renting 110–125cc Cambodian road law does not require a local licence below 125cc. Home car licence alone is not the real issue under road law for sub-125cc, but it may not satisfy insurers if it lacks motorcycle entitlement. Commonly rentable; many shops advertise 110–125cc bikes and light document checks. Weak to uncertain; travel/local policies may exclude if rider lacks a "valid" motorcycle licence.
Tourist with home motorcycle licence and 1949 IDP, renting 125cc+ Licence required. Best short-stay package reflected in UK guidance, but official guidance conflicts with Australian advice. Some tourist shops do rent 150cc bikes. Better than unlicensed riding, but still confirm rental and travel insurance in writing.
Tourist with no licence, renting any scooter Sub-125cc may be road-law lawful; 125cc+ is not. No strong recognition basis. Shops may still rent; anecdotal reports say checks can be minimal. Highest risk of claim denial and personal liability.
Long-term resident with Cambodian licence Strongest and least contestable position. Clear MPWT process for exchange or testing. More reputable long-term arrangements are easier to negotiate. Strongest insurance position, though policy wording still matters.

The main uncertainty in this subject is not the 125cc rule; that part of the law is fairly clear. The uncertainty lies in foreign-licence recognition above 125cc, the exact current roadside fine table in official English, and the mismatch between road-law legality and insurance wording. For that reason, the safest operational rule is stricter than what many rental shops allow: rent only the smallest bike your licence and insurance clearly support, or get the Cambodian licence first.
